Icebreaker⏱ 5 min · pairs

Two Truths, One Audience

In pairs: say the same true fact about the Autonomous AP case twice — once the way you'd say it to a skeptical CIO on the panel, once the way you'd say it to your own UST leadership. If both versions sound identical, one of them isn't actually speaking to its audience yet.

01Concept · 8 min

The Two-Audience Narrative

Every panel pitch actually has two audiences in the room at once, even when only one of them speaks: the panel itself, evaluating whether the solution is credible — and UST, evaluating whether this is a capability worth having built at all.

  • To the panel: the Complication is the client's problem — cost, risk, scale. The Answer has to earn trust in the room, on the spot.
  • To UST: the Complication is different — can we repeat this, sell it again, defend it competitively? The Answer is about capability and reusable IP, not just this one engagement.
  • Same evidence, two narratives. The numbers don't change; which consequence gets emphasized does.

  • A. They use different numbers
  • B. They share the same evidence but emphasize different consequences
  • C. The UST narrative is aspirational, not evidence-based

02Concept · 7 min

From Narrative to Deck

Real framework: Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 Rule — 10 slides, 20 minutes, 30-point minimum font. Constraints that force compression, not decoration.

  • A standard ROI-deck sequence maps directly onto SCQA: Situation → Complication → Answer (solution overview) → How It Works → Governance & Risk → ROI/TCO → Proof → The Ask.
  • 30-point-minimum font isn't a style rule — it forces you to cut every sentence down to the one thing that sentence is actually for. If it doesn't fit at 30pt, it's two ideas, not one.
  • The deck compresses the narrative from Lab 1. It does not replace the discipline of having written the narrative in full first.

03Concept · 7 min

Dual Narrative — Panel vs. UST, in Practice

Concretely, the same fact reframed for two audiences without changing the fact itself.

"2.2-month payback"
To the panel: your risk is small and short-lived. To UST: this pattern funds its own next iteration almost immediately — worth standardizing.
"5/5 governance tests pass"
To the panel: you don't have to trust us, you can verify us. To UST: this is a delivery methodology competitors can't easily match.
"11.9% stays fully manual"
To the panel: we're not overselling autonomy — the risky cases still get a human. To UST: honesty about limits is what makes the other 88% of the pitch credible.

Pop Quiz · 3

Where does the honest "catch" — the 11.9% that gets zero automation — actually belong in the deck?

  • A. Nowhere — leave it out, it undercuts the pitch
  • B. Stated explicitly, on the Proof slide, as "by design"
  • C. Only mentioned if the panel asks
  • D. In an appendix nobody presents
Answer: B. A credible ROI story states its own limits before being asked — that's what makes the rest of the number believable.
RBPanel Rubric · 5 min walkthrough

How the Panel Will Actually Score This

Five criteria, 1–4 each. Know this before you finish Lab 3, not after.

Narrative
Full prose, not fragments; Complication names both the visible and invisible problem.
Deck
Every number traceable to the narrative; the honest limit is stated, not hidden.
Governance evidence
Claims are backed by something tested, not just asserted.
Panel narrative
Leads with evidence and trust, not just the savings number.
UST narrative
Argues repeatability and differentiation — not a restatement of the panel pitch.
